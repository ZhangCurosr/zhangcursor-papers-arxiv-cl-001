# FERN: Cross-Benchmark Table Retrieval for Text-to-SQL with Frozen Retrievers and Learned Ranking

Arjun Rao arjun.rao01@sap.com

Nishant Gopinath nishant.gopinath@sap.com

November 2026

## Abstract

Text-to-SQL systems over large pooled catalogs must first retrieve the tables a question needs. We compare against the published table recall of RASL, which uses Claude 3.5 models to extract keywords and select tables, on pooled BIRD, Spider, and Fiben catalogs. FERN (Feature-Engineered Ranking with No generative LLM) instead fuses three frozen retrievers into 100 candidates, describes each with up to 120 features, and reorders them with LightGBM rankers fitted once on BIRD’s training questions. On Spider, where it was not fitted, its candidate pool alone reaches RASL’s published recall at fifteen tables (98.0), and the ranker raises the share of questions with every gold table in the top five (complete@5) from 83.9% to 95.6%. At five tables, FERN’s recall is within one point of RASL’s on Spider (96.7 vs 97.0) and on BIRD with its evidence hints (96.6 vs 97.5), and 8.2 points below on BIRD without them (89.3). On 121 real BEAVER questions, without BEAVER fitting, its recall at fifteen (82.1) falls between two BEAVER-trained retrievers (80.8, 84.5), but its complete@15 is below both (49.6 vs 54.5 and 62.0). On Fiben, it falls far short at fifteen tables (54.2 vs 69.2, diferent question sets), although its recall at fifty is numerically close to RASL’s question-only retrieval result (80.7 vs 79.4). On BIRD dev with evidence, non-retrieval features raise complete@5 by 7.7 points over retrieval signals alone. On BIRD dev, median latency is 208 ms per question on one A100, without hosted model calls.

Keywords: text-to-SQL; schema linking; table retrieval; learning to rank; hybrid retrieval; feature engineering

## 1 Introduction

A text-to-SQL system can write a faithful query only if it has the tables the query needs. Over a pooled catalog with hundreds of tables from many databases, a retrieval stage has to find those few tables first: a missing table makes the query wrong, and extra tables consume the generator’s context. This step, schema linking or table retrieval, increasingly uses large language models to extract keywords, rewrite questions, or read candidate schemas and choose (Pourreza & Rafiei, 2023; Talaei et al., 2024; Eben et al., 2025). We use RASL’s primary table-retrieval results (Eben et al., 2025) as a published reference on pooled BIRD, Spider, and Fiben catalogs. RASL retrieves schema entities with keywords extracted by Claude 3.5 Haiku and selects tables with Claude 3.5 Sonnet v2.

Generative linking has costs beyond accuracy. RASL uses hosted models for keyword extraction and table selection; its end-to-end input-token estimate is 1.85 US dollars per 100 BIRD queries, including 3 cents in the category it labels retrieval (Eben et al., 2025). In enterprise settings, sending schema and question text to an external service may not be acceptable. Retrieval research has long combined many weak relevance signals with a learned ranker (Qin & Liu, 2013; Burges, 2010), and such a ranker needs ingredients that can run locally: lexical search, of-the-shelf embedding models, and schema statistics.

This paper asks: how far can a ranker trained once on a public benchmark take table retrieval across pooled catalogs, using frozen retrievers and no generative model in the retrieval path? We answer with FERN (Feature-Engineered Ranking with No generative LLM). Three frozen retrievers propose 100 candidates; each candidate is described by features computed from the question, the catalog, and the retrievers’ scores; LightGBM rankers (Ke et al., 2017) fitted once on BIRD’s training questions order them. We compare with RASL’s published numbers on the same pooled catalogs, with no database identifier given, and apply the BIRD-fitted rankers unchanged to Spider, Fiben, and three pooled BEAVER warehouses (Chen et al., 2024b).

The results, in brief. At a nominal budget of five tables, FERN’s recall is within 0.3 points of RASL’s on Spider and 0.9 points on BIRD with evidence (BIRD’s per-question hints that name relevant columns and values); when FERN returns fifteen tables, it is about one point higher on both (on question-only BIRD it is 0.7 points below RASL’s BIRD figure). Spider is the cleanest of these comparisons: FERN was never fitted on it, no design decision used it, and it involves no evidence ambiguity. Without BIRD’s evidence hints, FERN’s recall at five falls by 7.3 points. FERN’s own candidate pool already reaches RASL’s Spider recall at fifteen (98.0); the ranker’s value lies mainly at small budgets and in complete table sets. On BEAVER, FERN’s recall at fifteen falls between two retrievers trained on BEAVER, but fewer questions get a complete table set (every gold table in the list). On Fiben, its recall is close to RASL’s question-only retrieval result at fifty tables, but substantially lower than RASL’s selected-table recall at fifteen (Section 7).

## Contributions.

1. FERN is a table retriever that combines three frozen retrievers with gradient-boosted rankers over schema-specific features, in three variants for catalogs with or without evidence, cell values, and column types. It runs in about 0.2 seconds per question on one GPU with no hosted model calls.

2. A comparison with published LLM-based schema linking (RASL) on its pooled BIRD, Spider, and Fiben catalogs. We made design choices on BIRD training data using rules stated in advance, with two documented exceptions; we fitted the rankers once; Spider and Fiben informed no design decisions.

3. An analysis, in the frozen configuration, of which feature groups matter at which table budget. On BIRD dev with evidence, rankers over retrieval signals alone or over the other features alone each reach about 98 recall at fifteen tables; adding the non-retrieval features to the retrieval signals raises complete@5 by 7.7 points. In the exploratory complete@5 ablations, removing database-support or join-graph features lowered the point estimate by 0.8 points each, with unadjusted intervals above zero; the other intervals touched or included zero.

4. Transfer results on a second benchmark, an opaque financial schema, and an enterprise bench mark with three warehouses, separating recall from complete-set recovery and showing, on these benchmarks, where a ranker trained on public data is enough and where it is not.

## 2 Related work

Schema linking with language models. Earlier text-to-SQL parsers linked question spans to schema elements by name and value matching inside the model (Guo et al., 2019; Wang, B. et al., 2020). RESDSQL ranks schema items with a learned cross-encoder before decoding (Li, H. et al., 2023), and CodeS combines schema-item classification with value retrieval (Li, H. et al., 2024). With large language models, linking became a prompting step: DIN-SQL links the schema with an LLM before generation (Pourreza & Rafiei, 2023) and CHESS retrieves entities and context in an LLM-driven pipeline (Talaei et al., 2024). Maamari et al. (2024) argue that strong reasoning models reduce the need for explicit schema linking when the database is known and small. These methods mostly assume a given database. Our setting pools many databases without identifying the target, as in RASL (Eben et al., 2025), which indexes schema entities with descriptions, retrieves with LLMextracted keywords, calibrates relevance on 200 training questions per dataset where a training split exists (not Fiben), and lets an LLM choose the tables. LinkAlign (Wang, Y. et al., 2025) also addresses linking across many databases.

Retrieval for large catalogs. CRUSH4SQL asks an LLM to hallucinate a minimal schema and retrieves real tables that match it (Kothyari et al., 2023). DBCopilot routes questions to databases and tables with a generative model trained on synthetic questions generated over the target databases (Wang, T. et al., 2025); in RASL’s Table 2, it reports 94.6 recall at fifteen on BIRD and 97.6 on Spider. Dense table retrieval embeds questions and tables in a shared space (Herzig et al., 2021). Chen, Zhang & Roth (2024a) show that join-aware retrieval matters when questions need several tables. CORE-T (Soliman et al., 2026) targets coherent multi-table retrieval over pooled catalogs without database identifiers and also evaluates on BEAVER; it reports table-selection F1 rather than recall@k on RASL’s catalogs, so we do not compare with it numerically. CSR-RAG (Singh et al., 2026) combines similarity to past questions on the target catalog, a schema knowl edge graph, and hypergraph ranking, without an LLM in retrieval; FERN uses no target-catalog history and is fitted once on a public benchmark. NIFT-SQL (Rao & Gopinath, 2026) fine-tunes a necessity scorer and a retriever on a customer’s own questions and fuses them; it asks what targetcatalog supervision buys. FERN asks the complementary question: what a ranker fitted once on public data delivers with no target-domain fitting. Both use database-level support features in a learned fusion; FERN difers in using only frozen retrievers, a larger schema-specific feature set, cross-benchmark transfer, and a comparison with LLM-based linking.

Learning to rank and hybrid retrieval. Learning to rank over hand-built relevance features is classic information retrieval (Qin & Liu, 2013), with gradient-boosted trees such as LambdaMART among the strongest rankers on such features (Burges, 2010; Qin, Z. et al., 2021). For tables, Zhang & Balog (2018) already rank web tables with supervised learning over lexical and semantic features; FERN difers in its target (the set of tables a SQL query needs), its pooled multi-database catalogs, and its evaluation of transfer across benchmarks. Hybrid lexical and dense retrieval is a standard first stage, commonly fused by reciprocal rank fusion (Cormack et al., 2009; Bruch et al., 2023). We claim no new ranking algorithm. The contribution is empirical: how far this recipe reaches against LLM-based linking, at which budgets, and where it stops.

## 3 Problem setting

Task. Given a natural-language question and a pooled catalog of tables from many databases, return a ranked list of tables such that the tables the gold SQL query uses appear near the top. The target database is not given.

## 4 Method

FERN has four stages.

1. Frozen retrieval. Each table is represented by a full card, table (database db): col1, col2 (natural name), ..., where underscores in the database name become spaces and a column’s natural-language name (BIRD’s and Spider’s column\_names) is added in parentheses when it difers from the stored name after removing spaces, underscores, and case; and by a name card, table words (database db), with camelCase and underscores split. Cards contain no types, keys, or values and are not truncated; BGE-large reads at most 512 tokens, so for very wide tables its signal sees only the first part of the card. Five signals score every table: BM25 (Robertson & Zaragoza, 2009) on full cards and on name cards (rank\_bm25 BM25Okapi, k1 = 1.5, b = 0.75; tokens are lower-cased alphanumeric runs after splitting camelCase and underscores, with no stemming or stop words); Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (Zhang, Y. et al., 2025) on full and name cards, with its built-in query instruction for questions; and BGE-large-en-v1.5 (Xiao et al., 2024) on full cards, with its retrieval prefix for questions. Embeddings are L2-normalized and compared by dot product, in bfloat16. The question text is the question followed by BIRD’s evidence in FERN-ev and the question alone otherwise.

2. Candidate pool. The pool score of a table is $\sum _ { s } 1 / ( 6 0 + \mathrm { r a n k } _ { s } )$ , summed over BM25, Qwen3, and BGE on full cards, with zero-based ranks over the whole catalog (Cormack et al., 2009). The 100 tables with the highest pool score are the candidates (average recall at 100 is 99.4 on BIRD dev). Ties are broken by array order.

3. Features. Table 3 describes each candidate’s features. We compute ranks, z-scores, percentiles, and gaps over the 100 candidates for each question.

4. Ranking. A LightGBM ranker with the lambdarank objective, trained with each question’s candidates as one group (pairwise gradients) and applied pointwise, scores the candidates, which are then sorted by that score.

Feature details. Tokens for lex, cov, and IDF are lower-cased alphanumeric runs after camelCase and underscore splitting, with a fixed list of about 60 function words removed and a final s stripped from words longer than three characters; IDF is log(N / (1 + df)) over the catalog’s tables. Fuzzy matching compares each question token of at least four characters with every catalog token by normalized Indel similarity (rapidfuzz). Literals are quoted strings of 2 to 60 characters, capitalized spans of the question with leading function words removed, and numbers. Cell values are indexed once per catalog from the databases’ SQLite files: up to 20,000 distinct non-null values per text column, lower-cased, 2 to 60 characters, matched exactly; and the minimum and maximum of numeric columns. A column is date-like if its type contains date or time, or its name contains date, year, or time; fixed regular expressions detect question types. PageRank (Page et al., 1999; damping 0.85, 30 iterations) and join distances (up to five hops, nine otherwise) use declared foreign keys, undirected, within each database. The top candidate has the highest pool score. The projection for emb is a PCA with 16 components fitted on every ninth candidate row of BIRD train (with evidence) and reused for all variants. We will release the complete feature code with the materials listed under Data and code availability. The pipeline is deterministic: rerunning it from scratch reproduced every reported retrieval number exactly.

Variants and fitting. Three rankers are fitted once on 9,394 BIRD training questions whose gold SQL parses and names a catalog table (RASL reports 9,427 training questions). FERN-ev uses the question with evidence and all 120 features. FERN-q uses the question alone and drops the evidence features and the evidence flag (116). FERN-schema also drops cell values, column types, and row counts (102) for catalogs whose values or types are not available to us. Labels are 1 for gold tables among the candidates and 0 otherwise. The authors set hyperparameters once before the development runs, using common LightGBM settings, and never tuned them: 600 trees, learning rate 0.05, 63 leaves, at least 50 rows per leaf, row subsampling 0.8 every iteration, column subsampling 0.7, L2 regularization 1.0, seed 0, other parameters at LightGBM defaults.

Which variant runs where. BIRD with evidence uses FERN-ev; BIRD without evidence and Spider use FERN-q (Spider has values but no evidence). Fiben and BEAVER use FERN-schema: our copies have no cell values, BEAVER provides no column types, and Fiben’s declared foreign keys (159) and BEAVER’s declared keys, where they exist, feed the graph features.

## 5 Protocol

Rules stated in advance. The candidate pool and the set of signals were chosen by rules fixed before running them and applied with five-fold cross-validation (folds by database) on a 3,000-question subset of BIRD train: the pool with the highest complete@100, with ties within 0.2 points going to fewer retrievers; and a signal kept only if removing it costs at least 0.3 points of complete@5 or complete@15, with a joint-removal check. The rule removed a best-column dense signal (Appendix A).

Two documented exceptions. (1) The pool rule selects Qwen3 + BGE (complete@100 98.4) over BM25 + Qwen3 + BGE (98.3). We overrode it to include lexical retrieval, arguing that exact matching complements dense retrieval for codes and abbreviations. The override was made after an earlier BEAVER transfer check, in a diferent configuration, had been seen, but before Spider and Fiben were scored; it should be read as informed by that check. (2) A fine-tuned decision model was evaluated on BIRD dev during development and left out of FERN because it added little (Appendix B). That decision used BIRD dev.

What each test set has informed. Spider and Fiben informed no decision. Fiben was evaluated with FERN-schema. Spider was evaluated with FERN-q and, after the method was frozen, with FERN-schema for the analysis without cell values in Section 6.1; neither Spider evaluation informed a design decision. That analysis also uses a fourth ranker, FERN-ev refitted on BIRD train without cell values, column types, or row counts. BIRD dev informed the second exception and the development studies of Appendix B. BEAVER informed the first exception. BIRD dev and BEAVER results are therefore development-exposed; Spider is the cleanest comparison with RASL. We exclude evaluation questions and labels from fitting; each question is available at inference, and catalogs are visible as the task requires: BM25 statistics, IDF, and the value index are built over the catalog being searched.

Comparison with RASL. We take RASL’s retrieval results from its published Tables 2 and 5 and its cost figures from its cost analysis (Eben et al., 2025); we did not rerun it. We use the same named development splits and reported catalog counts for BIRD and Spider; exact question-ID and gold-label parity with RASL is not verified. Fiben uses a diferent question set. Table 2 lists the input diferences.

Leakage, by provenance. Table 4 lists every input and the stages that see it. The feature code receives the question, the evidence (FERN-ev only), the catalog, the cell values, and the frozen scores; candidate identities come from the whole catalog, and the gold SQL, gold tables, and gold database are never passed to it. Labels are attached after features are computed. PCA and the rankers are fitted only on BIRD train. As a coarse sanity check, not a proof, no single feature separates gold from non-gold candidates on BIRD dev with an AUC above 0.98; the strongest, the pool score’s gap to the best candidate, reaches 0.978, as expected of the main retrieval signal.

At a nominal budget of five tables, FERN is 0.3 points below RASL’s published recall on Spider and 0.9 points below on BIRD with evidence. When FERN returns fifteen tables, it is 1.1 points higher on both; RASL returns at most fifteen, so this compares a full list of fifteen with a possibly shorter one. Whether RASL used BIRD’s evidence is not stated: its error-analysis examples (Figures 3 and 5 and Appendix E of Eben et al., 2025) show questions with evidence-like clauses appended, which supports FERN-ev as the closer comparison. Without evidence, FERN’s recall at five falls to 89.3, 8.2 points below RASL, and its recall at fifteen to 97.1, 0.7 points below. For deployments without per-question evidence hints, the question-only row is more relevant.

FERN’s candidate pool alone already reaches RASL’s Spider recall at fifteen (98.0) and is above every baseline without LLM table selection in RASL’s comparison at fifteen tables, on Spider and on BIRD with evidence (the question-only BIRD pool, 94.2, is below DBCopilot’s 94.6). Much of the gap between earlier non-LLM baselines and RASL is thus closed by the frozen candidate pool; the ranker earns its place at small budgets and on complete sets (Table 6). ## 6.3 Where the signal comes from

We split the 120 features into retrieval signals (the score, agree, and db families, 53 features) and the other families (67), with the same hyperparameters. We fitted each ranker on all of BIRD train and evaluated it on BIRD dev; separately, we ran five-fold cross-validation grouped by database within BIRD train (all 9,394 questions). The PCA projection of the emb family, fitted once on all of BIRD train, is shared across folds; it is unsupervised but sees the held-out folds’ unlabeled rows, and the retrieval-only ranker does not use it, so the cross-validation is a development diagnostic rather than a fully held-out estimate. We also remove one family at a time (BIRD dev). The split is not perfectly clean: some of the other features use the pool’s top candidates as reference points (coverage beyond the top table, join distance to it, the entropy of pool scores).

## 7 Discussion

BIRD and Spider. On these benchmarks, whose table and column names are mostly readable English, frozen retrievers already place almost every gold table in the candidate pool. The study does not isolate readability as the variable that explains the diference from Fiben. What remains is ordering the candidates. At a nominal budget of five, FERN’s recall is 0.3 points below RASL’s published value on Spider and 0.9 points below on BIRD with evidence; the comparison does not isolate the contribution of LLM selection. We do not know why RASL is ahead on BIRD at five tables; one hypothesis is the LLM’s ability to judge which of several plausible tables a question needs, another is RASL’s use of BIRD’s column and value descriptions.

Evidence. BIRD’s evidence hints often name the exact columns a question needs. With evidence, FERN’s recall at five is 7.3 points higher (96.6 vs 89.3). The 7.3-point diference compares two separately fitted variants that difer in both the query text and the evidence-specific features; it is not decomposed between them. For deployments without per-question evidence hints, the questiononly results are more relevant, including the 8.2-point gap at five tables to RASL’s published number.

Opaque schemas. Fiben’s tables have run-together uppercase names (for example, ACCOUNTSPAYABLE ANDACCRUEDLIABILITIES), 2.5 columns per table, one database, and almost four gold tables per question. Reranking the pool by BM25 and the pool itself both reach only 39 recall at fifteen; FERN improves on its pool by 15 points but remains far below RASL. The results expose limitations in candidate retrieval and in ranking under a small output budget. The fused pool is weak: 67.0 recall at fifty tables, against 79.4 for RASL’s question-only retriever and 90.6 for its pool using the question plus LLM-extracted keywords (Eben et al., 2025, Table 5), and its average recall at 100 is capped at 82.8; standalone BM25 over the whole catalog reaches only 7.0 at fifteen.

Fiben has only 152 tables, so fifty is a third of the catalog. After reranking its 100 candidates, FERN reaches 80.7 recall when returning fifty tables, but only 54.2 when returning fifteen, a drop of 26.5 points. RASL’s question-only configuration reports 79.4 recall for its initial fifty-table pool and 67.5 after LLM selection with a maximum output of fifteen, a drop of 11.9 points. These figures suggest that retaining relevant tables under a small output budget is a substantial dificulty for FERN. They do not isolate the efect of LLM selection, because the systems use diferent question sets, embeddings, and retrieval representations; RASL retrieves individual schema entities with Cohere embeddings. Fiben also difers from BIRD and Spider in several ways at once: its names are unsegmented, our copy has no cell values, it has a single database (which removes discrimination between databases but preserves within-database ranking signals), and its questions need many tables. We cannot tell which of these matters.

Enterprise use. On the 121 real dw questions, with no BEAVER question used for fitting (although BEAVER results informed one design decision; Section 5), FERN’s recall at fifteen fell between the two target-trained point estimates, while its complete@15 was lower than both. Both baselines were trained on BEAVER’s synthetic questions (Table 8); a retriever fine-tuned this way could enter FERN as another signal, which we have not tested. We see public-data fitting and target-catalog supervision as stages of a deployment: a ranker fitted on public data first, supervision on the target catalog once data exists.

## 8 Limitations

• Published reference numbers. We did not rerun RASL; its per-question results are unavailable, and its inputs difer (Table 2): it uses BIRD’s descriptions, not cell values, and returns at most N tables. Diferences from RASL carry no interval. The Fiben question sets difer.

• Development exposure. BIRD dev and BEAVER informed two design decisions (Section 5); Spider and Fiben did not.

• Supervision. The rankers are fitted on 9,394 labeled BIRD questions, far more than RASL’s 200 calibration questions. FERN avoids target-domain fitting, not supervision. Pretraining of the frozen embedders on public benchmarks cannot be ruled out, as for any pretrained model.

• Evidence and values. FERN’s best BIRD numbers use evidence and cell values. Without evidence, recall at five is 8.2 points below RASL’s published number.

• Gold labels. Gold-table extraction changes 1.6% of BIRD dev gold sets, the same order as the diferences from RASL; RASL does not describe its extraction. BIRD’s gold SQL itself contains annotation errors (Wretblad et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2026), which can afect both FERN’s and RASL’s reported recall.

• Candidate ceiling. Gold tables outside the 100 candidates cannot be recovered (average recall at 100 is 99.4 on BIRD dev and 82.8 on Fiben).

• End-to-end scope. We evaluate table retrieval only, not database routing or the SQL generated from the retrieved tables.

• Benchmarks. We evaluate RASL’s three benchmarks and one enterprise benchmark. Larger enterprise suites with thousands of tables need candidate-pool decisions of their own and are left to future work.

## 9 Conclusion

Against RASL’s published results on pooled catalogs, rankers fitted once on BIRD over frozen retrievers come within one point of its published recall at five tables on Spider and on BIRD with evidence, at about 0.2 seconds per question with no hosted model calls; without evidence, the gap on BIRD at five tables is eight points. On Spider, the candidate pool of frozen retrievers (BM25, Qwen3 and BGE) alone already reaches RASL’s recall at fifteen, and the ranker’s main contribution is complete table sets at small budgets. On the real questions of an enterprise benchmark, the rankers’ recall at fifteen falls between that of two retrievers trained on it, while their complete@15 is lower than both. On Fiben, their recall is numerically close to RASL’s question-only retrieval result at fifty tables but substantially lower than RASL’s selected tables at fifteen; both candidate retrieval and ranking under a small budget remain limitations. FERN was competitive in reported recall on Spider and on BIRD with evidence, which makes it a strong baseline on these benchmarks; Fiben shows where it is not enough.
