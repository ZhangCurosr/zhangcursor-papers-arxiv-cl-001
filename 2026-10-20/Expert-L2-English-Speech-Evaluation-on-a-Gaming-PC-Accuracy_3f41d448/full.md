# Expert L2 English Speech Evaluation on a Gaming PC: Accuracy, Fluency, and Prosody with XLS-R, XEUS, and PhoneticXeus

Richard Rose | HUFS | richard.rose@hufs.ac.kr

## Abstract

oollim is a locally executable system for evaluating L2 English pronunciation accuracy, fluency, and prosody on a consumer computer. The system combines pretrained XLS-R, XEUS, and PhoneticXeus representations with reference-text features and ridge regressions trained on SpeechOcean762, the Korean Learners’ L2 Speech Corpus (English), J-AESOP, and their combinations. On 305 independently held-out recordings assessed by three expert raters, several ridge configurations substantially outperform archived Azure API scores obtained in January 2026 in both Spearman correlation and quadratic weighted kappa across all three constructs. These results support expert speech evaluation on consumer hardware using pretrained representations and construct-specific regressions, without neural-model fine-tuning or calibration on the private evaluation data.

## 1 Introduction

Pronunciation evaluation requires distinct judgments of accuracy, fluency, and prosody. Accuracy concerns the realization of expected consonants and vowels [1, 2]; fluency concerns speech continuity, including speed, pauses, and repairs [3, 4]; prosody concerns rhythm, stress, intonation, and phrasing [5, 6]. These related dimensions inform pronunciation judgments but are not interchangeable [2, 7]. Segmental accuracy concerns distinctions between speech sounds, whereas intelligibility concerns what listeners understand and comprehensibility their perceived processing dificulty; a noticeable accent need not prevent understanding [8]. An accuracy score does not directly measure intelligibility. Fluency research distinguishes underlying processing eficiency, observable timing, and perceived smoothness [9]. Speech rate, pause placement and duration, and repairs contribute diferently to listener judgments, so fluency is not simply speaking quickly [3, 4]. Prosody organizes speech through lexical stress, prominence, phrasing, and intonation, with pitch, duration, and intensity providing interacting cues [5, 6]. Appropriate stress can improve listeners’ recall and evaluations of a speaker [10]; contextual appropriateness therefore matters alongside acoustic variation. Although their acoustic correlates overlap, the three constructs require separate targets and useful measurements for educators; the scores need not come from the same representation combination or scoring function. XLS-R, XEUS, and PhoneticXeus provide complementary sources of speech information. This study examines whether these existing models, combined with modest supervised scoring functions, can support expert L2 English evaluation on an ordinary gaming PC. With computationally expensive pretraining supplied by the original model developers, the contribution is the selection and combination of their representations, reference-text evidence, and corpus-specific assessment supervision. The central evidence is an external evaluation on independently collected, privately held recordings, while training-corpus development results serve model selection and reproducibility and are provided in the repository. The external comparison includes Azure pronunciation assessment [11], seven ridge-training families, and two human agreement baselines, with both rank association and numerical agreement measured. This design separates three questions: whether scores rank recordings similarly to human judgments, whether they agree on the numerical scale, and how that agreement compares with agreement among the human raters [12].

## 2 Speech models and related work

Automated assessment has long combined acoustic evidence with supervised mappings to human judgments. SpeechRater derives pronunciation, fluency, and other proficiency features from recognized speech and uses learned scoring models [13]. Neural assessment extends this approach by learning representations and their relationship to rated performance. GOPT uses phonetic posterior features and canonical phone identities in a transformer trained jointly on phone-, word-, and utterance-level human annotations [14]. On SpeechOcean762, the 2,500-utterance training and test partitions provide the same assessment targets on diferent recordings [15, 14]. The scoring model is trained on human ratings and evaluated against ratings of held-out recordings. Kim et al. adapt wav2vec 2.0 and HuBERT with CTC, then combine contextual representations and reference text in a bidirectional LSTM scoring model, evaluating against expert scores on separate partitions of Korean learner speech and SpeechOcean762 [16]. Cai et al. connect hierarchical neural assessment to applied-linguistics constructs, adapting scoring toward human judgments of segmental, suprasegmental, and temporal performance [2]. Saito et al. use regression ove phonological, prosodic, and temporal features to predict comprehensibility, testing generalization on additional recordings and an interview task [17]. These studies train scoring models against human ratings and assess multiple dimensions or transfer to new data, although diferences in targets, rating panels, and metrics prevent direct numerical comparison with oollim. XLS-R extends cross-lingual self-supervised speech representation learning [18]; XEUS learns multilingual representations across thousands of languages [19]; PhoneticXeus adds phone recognition and supplies hidden representations and phone evidence [20]. In oollim their pretrained weights remain unchanged, while ridge regression learns regularized linear mappings to assessment labels [21]. oollim combines these representations with reference-phone diagnostics and construct-specific regressions trained on one or more corpora, then evaluates them on an independently collected passage-reading cohort to test transfer beyond holding out recordings within a training corpus. Earlier neural-head, temporal, and acoustic-feature experiments motivated the ridge baseline; the evaluation does not cover every neural assessment architecture. The comparison therefore focuses on the external transfer of compact scoring models.

## 3 Method

## 3.1 Audio, reference text, and representations

Whole-recording embeddings and phone diagnostics derived from required reference text provide the scoring inputs. FFmpeg [22] decodes audio-bearing containers; a common preprocessing path averages channels to mono and resamples to 16 kHz. The full recording is processed as one utterance, without averaging separately scored sentences. Each encoder adapter returns frame embeddings and valid-frame lengths or masks, with XLS-R 300M, XEUS, and PhoneticXeus each providing 1,024-dimensional frame representations in the configurations used here [18, 19, 20]. Statistics pooling [23], using masked temporal means and population standard deviations, with $1 0 ^ { - 5 }$ added to the variance before the square root, yields 2,048-dimensional utterance vectors. Model revisions and implementation checksums are pinned in the repository. A pinned CMU pronunciation dictionary supplies ARPAbet reference pronunciations [24]. ARPAbet–IPA correspondences are documented in CMU dictionary tools [25]; the versioned mapping in oollim converts dictionary phones to the PhoneticXeus token inventory, including explici diphthong splitting and unstressed-vowel handling. PhoneticXeus provides frame-level phone probabilities and a greedy connectionist temporal classification (CTC) sequence [20, 26]. Seventeen diagnostics summarize phone probabilities and entropy [27], blank behavior, predicted and expected phone rates, reference coverage, relative sequence length, edit operations [28], and CTC negative log-likelihoods normalized by frame and reference-token counts [26]. The CTC calculation sums valid paths for the expected phone sequence rather than assigning a single forced alignment [26]. These features combine pronunciation, timing, and uncertainty evidence; they are not error-free phonetic diagnoses. Out-of-vocabulary references are rejected explicitly. The evaluated accuracy configuration uses XEUS + PhoneticXeus + IPA diagnostics, whereas fluency and prosody use XLS-R + XEUS. The scoring pipeline can select a representation combination and training family separately for each construct.

## 3.2 Training corpora and label mappings

Three research corpora supply assessment labels mapped to a common 0–10 range. SpeechOcean762 supplies utterance accuracy, fluency, and prosodic ratings on the 0–10 scale. The oficial SpeechOcean762 training partition is divided by speaker into 2,000 training and 500 development utterances; the oficial test partition is excluded. The Korean Learners’ L2 Speech Corpus (English) [29] is distributed as AIHub dataset 71463 [30]. Eligible Training PRN recordings yield 2,699 training and 636 development examples. The corpus rubric rates pronunciation accuracy (segment clarity and errors) and prosodic fluency (stress, rhythm, intonation, rate, and pauses) on 1–5 scales [29]. The supplied articulation score trains accuracy, while the prosodic-fluency score trains both fluency and prosody after mapping with 2.5(y − 1). J-AESOP [31] supplies 180 Japanese L2 speakers reading the North Wind and Sun passage (task 6\_01). Deterministic speaker partitions allocate 108 recordings to training, 36 to development, and 36 to a reserved partition; native controls are excluded. Ratings from 16 raters across three sections are averaged per construct. Segment ratings supply accuracy, alongside fluency and prosody; $1 0 ( y - 1 ) / 9$ maps the 1–10 source scale to 0–10. Partitions are speaker-disjoint within each corpus to separate development from fitting [32]. In the Korean Learners’ L2 Speech Corpus (English), recorder identities linked by identical decoded audio are grouped before splitting; duration and dictionary-coverage checks determine eligibility. Exact sampling rules, audio audits, mappings, and development results are retained in the reproduction package. Equal numerical ranges do not establish score equivalence [33]; the shared prosody/fluency target does not provide an independent fluency annotation. The mappings permit joint fitting while retaining these diferences in supervision.

## 3.3 Ridge fitting and family selection

Seven training families compare single-corpus and mixed-corpus supervision. The single-corpus families are speechocean, korean, and aesop; korean abbreviates the Korean Learners’ L2 Speech Corpus (English) in model identifiers. The pairs are koreanocean, aesopocean, and koreanaesop, while the three-corpus family is aesopkoreanocean. In the matched family procedure, a recording from corpus c receives weight $w _ { i } = 1 / ( K n _ { c } )$ where K is the number of included corpora and $n _ { c }$ their respective training counts. Training-only weighted means and standard deviations standardize features [34]; a block of width $d _ { b }$ receives gain $\sqrt { 2 0 4 8 / d _ { b } }$ . Coordinates with standard deviation below $1 0 ^ { - 8 }$ use scale 1. Each construct fits a weighted ridge objective [21, 34]

$$
\operatorname* { m i n } _ { \beta , b } \quad \sum _ { i } w _ { i } ( y _ { i } - z _ { i } ^ { \top } \beta - b ) ^ { 2 } + \alpha \| \beta \| _ { 2 } ^ { 2 } ,\tag{1}
$$

with an unpenalized intercept. Penalties {0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100} are compared using mean development Spearman correlation over the included corpora; ties prefer lower mean MAE and then larger regularization, keeping selection on development data [32]. Multi-output array storage does not couple the separate squared-loss objectives. All seven ridge families were scored for the present study. The deployed SpeechOcean ridge used an earlier tuning procedure, including development accuracy MAE, and its scores were carried forward from the first evaluation round rather than replaced with matched-refit predictions. The retained deployment is version speechocean-stella-v2; the other six families use the matched seven-families-v1 heads. Request metadata are retained where available; the original SpeechOcean scores were imported rather than regenerated. Public-development selection retained koreanocean for accuracy and aesopkoreanocean for fluency and prosody; these ranking-oriented research selections are distinct from the column maxima in the external table and are not claimed to maximize external QWK. The representation sensitivity analysis is separate from the fixed-combination external evaluation. A sensitivity check of all 315 family–construct–representation configurations on public development data retained the overall construct-specific selections under the 0.01 Spearman improvement threshold and corpus-level degradation and uncertainty checks recorded before fitting in the October 18, 2026 ablation protocol; the full protocol and results are provided in the reproduction materials. All coeficient estimation, preprocessing fitting, and score calibration exclude private evaluation ratings.

## 3.4 Local execution and capacity

Local operation was verified on consumer hardware using CPU inference. On the Windows gaming PC (Intel Core i7-11700K, 32 GB physical RAM, Ubuntu under WSL2 with approximately 24 GiB available RAM and 8 GiB swap), an archived 120-second capacity test completed in approximately 169 seconds, reaching 22.52 GiB peak process resident memory and 5.51 GiB peak system-wide swap use. The installed RTX 3080 was not required for that inference test. A separate Apple Silicon installation test processed an 8.21-second public recording in 42.13 seconds with four CPU threads, including decoding, all three encoders, phone features, and scoring. Software versions for the same WSL installation are recorded in the appendices. These single-run checks establish operation rather than representative throughput; the released duration limit is 120 seconds, and long recordings can require substantial RAM and swap.

## 4 External evaluation

## 4.1 Independent recordings and ratings

The collection comprised university EFL learners from China (n = 98), Korea (n = 101), and Japan (n = 107), who recorded the same “Please Call Stella” passage on personal smartphones. One recording was excluded because of its length, leaving 305 samples. The three university professors had approximately 20–30 years of teaching experience, including pronunciation instruction, Cambridge English speaking assessment, and high-stakes speech rating. Based in those countries, the raters calibrated their judgments on separate recordings spanning proficiency levels, discussing discrepancies to establish shared criteria for accuracy, fluency, and prosody. Each professor then rated every recording independently in randomized order, blinded to participant identity and automated scores using a 0–100 scale in five-point increments. The private evaluation recordings and ratings were held out from model training, hyperparameter tuning, and score calibration. The original human ratings and Azure API scores are archival; the seven ridge families were evaluated for the present study against those existing ratings. Azure scores were obtained in January 2026 using the en-US locale, with the Stella reference text supplied and each complete recording assessed as a whole. The returned accuracy, fluency, and prosody scores were retained. The service revision and remaining request options were not retained in the supplied evaluation archive; the comparison therefore concerns those recorded API outputs rather than a newly standardized Azure benchmark.

## 4.2 Metrics and human baselines

Evaluation distinguishes rank consistency from numerical agreement [35, 12]. Spearman’s ρ measures rank association [36]. Quadratic weighted kappa (QWK) measures chance-corrected agreement with squared categorydistance penalties [37]; this study uses fixed integer categories 0–100 and half-up rounding. Model outputs are multiplied by ten; no calibration is fitted. Predictions are clipped to 0–100 for QWK, including bootstrap replicates, whereas Spearman retains raw predictions. A reference mean requires all three human scores; partial means are not substituted. Human–Human pairwise agreement averages the three pairwise statistics, whereas Human–Human consensus agreement averages each rater’s agreement with the mean of the other two, never including that rate in their own reference. For each system, the three-rater row compares the system’s scores with the mean of all humans, while the two-rater row averages the three separate agreement statistics against the three possible two-human means. Statistics are averaged, not observations or reference means pooled. The two-rater system row and Human–Human consensus row therefore provide the appropriate reference-size comparison when recomputed on the same recordings. Percentile 95% intervals resample speaker clusters jointly, recomputing all component statistics within each replicate [38, 39]. The supplementary marginal intervals retain the supplied evaluation export (2,000 replicates, seed 42). Additional paired comparisons use 200,000 bootstrap replicates (seed 42), subtracting statistics within each replicate on the same sampled recordings. An independent 200,000-replicate run (seed 2026) produced the same interval-exclusion decisions for all contrasts. Each unique recording URL is treated as one speaker. Both percentile 95% intervals and Bonferroni-adjusted intervals across all 90 reported diferences are supplied: eight systems versus human consensus and seven ridges versus Azure, for three constructs and two metrics. These post-evaluation comparisons condition on the observed raters and fitted models; adjustment covers the reported contrasts, not the preceding model search. The appendices report all reference comparisons and intervals for the same 305 recordings. Matched reference sizes and cohorts make the human and model estimates comparable.

## 5 Results

Table 1 shows that every ridge family exceeds Azure in rank association across all three constructs, with mixed-corpus families generally strongest. Model correlations also exceed the human consensus baseline, but numerical agreement presents a diferent pattern: the leading accuracy model exceeds the human QWK baseline, whereas fluency and prosody remain below the human QWK baseline. Several families outperform Azure on both metrics, although the advantage is not universal for QWK. No family leads both metrics across all constructs, supporting construct-specific scoring choices. Across all 90 contrasts, Bonferroni-adjusted paired intervals remain above zero for every ridge–Azure Spearman diference and for koreanocean accuracy QWK versus human consensus. aesopocean, koreanaesop, and aesopkoreanocean also exceed human-consensus rank agreement across all three constructs under that adjustment; korean does so for prosody. No ridge has a positive adjusted QWK diference from human consensus for fluency or prosody. Full diferences and score-distribution summaries appear in the appendices; external family rankings remain descriptive.

## 6 Discussion

The results favor selecting scorers by construct and intended use. Public-development selection prioritizes rank correlation; users seeking numerical feedback should also consider QWK, for which the external table favors koreanocean accuracy and speechocean fluency/prosody. Those external preferences remain descriptive rather than independently validated deployment choices. Several mixed-corpus families have higher correlations but lower QWK values than single-corpus families, so scorer selection depends on whether the intended use requires ranking, numerical agreement, or both; no single family needs to generate all three constructs. Azure scores average 23.63, 27.88, and 10.57 points above the three-human means for accuracy, fluency, and prosody, respectively; these ofsets help explain low QWK despite positive rank associations. No correction was fitted to remove those ofsets. XEUS alone approaches the retained combinations on public-development macro correlation (gaps of 0.0017 for accuracy, 0.0016 for fluency, and 0.0041 for prosody in the selected families), so the sweep does not establish a substantial benefit from every additional encoder. A lighter XEUS-only system warrants separate external evaluation; the present private results apply to the evaluated combinations. Korean-only fluency and prosody share features and targets, so their predictions do not constitute independent learning of two constructs. Human baselines also answer diferent questions, with agreement against a two-rater consensus generally higher than agreement with one other human because averaging reduces some rater-specific variation. On the same reference pairs and recordings, leading model point estimates exceed the Human–Human consensus baseline for rank association across constructs and for accuracy QWK, whereas fluency and prosody QWK remain below that baseline. The advantage over human agreement therefore depends on the construct and metric, with paired uncertainty reported in the appendices. External family rankings are descriptive rather than prospectively validated selections [32]. Agreement with human ratings supports a particular score interpretation, rather than every educational use of the scores [40]. The software, trained scoring weights, model configurations, and reproduction materials are available at https://github.com/cburst/oollim, subject to the licenses documented in the repository. Practical scorer selection should therefore consider the target construct, agreement metric, and teaching context.

## 7 Limitations

The evaluation supports conclusions about one passage and three university EFL settings. Recording-level scores permit paired analysis, but URL-based grouping assumes one recording per speaker. The cohort excludes one long recording, and the 120-second operational limit constrains input coverage. Score-scale mappings do not establish equivalent constructs: the Korean Learners’ L2 Speech Corpus (English) supplies shared prosody/fluency supervision, so diferences among training families cannot be attributed solely to additional data. Speaker-disjoint fitting also cannot resolve possible upstream exposure of PhoneticXeus to learner corpora. The Azure service revision and remaining request options are unavailable, limiting exact replication of that comparator. The public-development ablation used a recorded selection rule, but neither bootstrap intervals nor contrast adjustment account for the entire model-search process. Broader claims require additional passages, populations, and intended uses [40].

## 8 Conclusion

XLS-R, XEUS, and PhoneticXeus, combined with construct-specific ridge scoring, support local evaluation of L2 English accuracy, fluency, and prosody on the tested consumer configuration. The external passage evaluation shows strong rank association with human ratings, with every ridge family exceeding correlations from the archived January 2026 Azure API outputs across all three constructs. Numerical agreement varies by construct and training family and identifies diferent leading scorers from rank association, supporting the reporting of both metrics for each construct. The additional public-development ablation retained the overall construct-specific configurations under the 0.01 improvement rule. Additional passages and populations are needed to establish the scope of comparative human-agreement claims.

## B Paired diferences and score distributions

The same 305 complete recordings were resampled jointly 200,000 times (seed 42), treating each distinct recording URL as one speaker. Human consensus agreement averages each rater’s agreement with the other two; system agreement averages comparisons against those same three reference pairs. Diferences are recomputed within each replicate. Intervals are percentile approximations; adjusted endpoints use probabilities 0.025/90 and 1 − 0.025/90, covering 90 reported contrasts. A second 200,000-replicate run (seed 2026) reproduced all 90 interval-exclusion decisions; adjusted endpoints difered by at most 0.00367. Each adjusted tail now contains approximately 56 draws, although percentile intervals remain approximations. The supplementary marginal intervals above retain the original 2,000-replicate export. These post-evaluation comparisons condition on the panel and fitted models, withou correcting for prior model exploration. All 48 system point estimates match the supplied export to numerical precision. Private scores were used only for evaluation; no coeficients or calibration were fitted.

## D Korean supervision and representation sensitivity

## D.1 Korean scoring and target mapping

Han, Chung, and Kim [29] define two 1–5 ratings: pronunciation accuracy and prosodic fluency. Accuracy addresses segmental clarity and errors; prosodic fluency combines stress, rhythm, intonation, speed, and pausing. The stored fields are articulationScore and prosodyScore. The second field supplies both fluency and prosody targets in the present implementation. Mapping 2.5(y −1) produces 0, 2.5, 5, 7.5, and 10 from the five integer levels; mapping does not equate the rubric with SpeechOcean or create an independent fluency annotation. Korean-only heads use the same features and targets for both constructs, yielding identical predictions in the evaluated configuration.

## D.2 Ablation protocol

The protocol was recorded before fitting on October 18, 2026 (repository: results/ablation-mac-v1/PROTOCOL. md). The completed public-development sweep crossed seven training families, three constructs, and all 15 nonempty subsets of four feature blocks, giving 315 configurations and 945 corpus-level comparisons. X denotes XEUS, R XLS-R, P PhoneticXeus embeddings, and I the 17 phone diagnostics; I still requires PhoneticXeus inference, so omitting P does not necessarily avoid that encoder. Existing speaker-disjoint partitions, corpus-balanced weights, training-only standardization, and block gains were preserved. Each configuration selected a penalty from {0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100} using equal-weight mean Spearman across all three development corpora, breaking ties by lower macro MAE and then larger penalty. This difers from the earlier included-corpus tuning; no corpus in these tables is an untouched cross-corpus test. Private recordings and ratings were excluded from fitting and selection.

Each candidate was compared with the fixed-feature baseline refitted under the same sweep protocol (X+P+I for accuracy, X+R otherwise). Candidates within 0.01 of the numerical best were shortlisted. Promotion required macro Spearman gain at least 0.01, no corpus correlation loss exceeding 0.02, no corpus MAE increase exceeding 0.10 on the 0–10 scale, and a positive lower 95% paired speaker-bootstrap bound (2,000 draws, seed 42). Fewer blocks were preferred where benefits were inconclusive. These screening gates do not constitute correction for all search comparisons. Deployed heads were not automatically replaced.

The decision table distinguishes numerical maxima from candidates passing the complete gate. Two AESOP-only alternatives passed: X+P for fluency and X+P+I for prosody. ## F External rater backgrounds and procedure

The original human-evaluation study reports three experienced university raters. The Korea-based rater had more than 20 years of teaching experience, including a decade of university pronunciation instruction and speaking assessment. The Japan-based rater had nearly 30 years of teaching experience and more than 10 years as a Cambridge English speaking assessor. The China-based rater had nearly 20 years of experience, including high-stakes speech rating and university speaking assessment in an international joint-degree program. All three held professorial positions, as confirmed by the study author.

Before evaluating the source recordings, the raters jointly assessed separate Stella recordings spanning proficiency levels and discussed discrepancies to establish shared criteria for segmental accuracy, fluency, and prosody. They then scored the 306 source recordings independently in randomized order, blinded to participant identity and automated scores. Ratings used a 0–100 scale in five-point increments, with communicative-efectiveness anchors labelled “High Intelligibility” above 85 and “Low Intelligibility” below 40. These are the documented general scale anchors, not a newly constructed analytic rubric for each dimension. The original study’s authors served as its raters; their ratings were reused as the external reference for the present model evaluation. One recording was subsequently excluded for excessive duration, leaving the matched 305-recording cohort. Human calibration used separate recordings and did not fit or calibrate any automated scorer.
