# Phonologically Informed Tokenization for German Speech Recognition: A Cross-Domain Study

Christopher Witzl Tobias Bocklet Korbinian Riedhammer

Technische Hochschule Nürnberg Georg Simon Ohm, Nürnberg, Germany {christopher.witzl, tobias.bocklet, korbinian.riedhammer}@th-nuernberg.de

## Abstract

German is a morphologically rich language whose syllable structure is exceptionally well predicted by the Knuth–Liang hyphenation algorithm. We ask whether phonologically informed tokenization can serve as a com petitive target for end-to-end speech recogni tion. We compare three tokenizer families on the Omnilingual ASR wav2vec 2.0 backbone fine-tuned with CTC: the pretrained multilin gual character inventory, a data-driven Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) over orthography, and phonologically informed units from Pyphen syllabification and grapheme-to-phoneme con version. Across 40 fine-tunes, we evaluate on three German test sets spanning orthogonal shifts: in-domain read speech, dialectal spon taneous speech, and standard-German sponta neous speech. In-domain, all phonologically informed tokenizers match BPE and the multi lingual character baseline on both WER and CER. Under domain shift the picture splits along vocabulary size rather than the linguis tic axis of variation: at small vocabularies, syllable-aware tokenization improves on dialectal speech, where phonetic surface forms vary but syllable structure is preserved, and stays ahead on spontaneous speech, where new word forms violate vocabulary closure. A phonemelevel confusion analysis further shows that all tokenizers commit the same canonical functionword errors, indicating that the acoustic encoder, not the tokenizer, dominates the error topology. Our findings suggest that tokenizer choice may depend on the vocabulary budget as much as on the distribution shift expected at deployment rather than reducing to a single universal optimum.

## 1 Introduction

Modern end-to-end automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems built on self-supervised pretraining (Baevski et al., 2020) couple an acoustic encoder with an output tokenizer that determines the units the network must predict. The de facto default has converged on two choices: a data-driven Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) (Sennrich et al., 2016) fitted to the training transcripts, or a fixed multilingual character inventory inherited from the pretrained ASR model (Omnilingual ASR team et al., 2025). Both are phonologically agnostic. The first treats character n-grams as merge candidates without regard to syllable or morpheme structure; the second models every character of every supported script as an independent unit, leaving word-level structure to the acoustic encoder. The choice is not cosmetic: the tokenizer fixes which strings the network can emit, how many target units per word the CTC objective must align, and how the model degrades on word-forms unseen in training.

This convergence is convenient, but it hides a methodological question with real downstream consequences: do phonologically informed tokenizers<sup>1</sup>, those that align with the syllabic and phonemic structure of the target language, offer something that data-driven and multilingual defaults cannot? German is a natural test case: it is morphologically rich (productive compounding, inflection), it has substantial dialectal variation, and large-scale German ASR benchmarks (Common Voice (Ardila et al., 2020), Spoken Wikipedia Corpus (Köhn et al., 2016)) coexist with structurally different out-of-distribution (OOD) corpora, namely dialectal spontaneous speech (BAS RVG 1, Burger and Schiel, 1998) and standard-German spontaneous dialogues (Verbmobil, Burger et al., 2000). The language thus offers two distinct OOD axes against which tokenizer behavior can be probed: RVG 1 varies the phonetic surface (a largely shared lexicon realized across nine dialect regions), while Verbmobil varies the lexical inventory (standard-German phonetics but negotiation-dialogue vocabulary including hesitations, discourse particles, and colloquial verb forms unseen at training time).

Contributions. We make three contributions. (1) We assemble and train 40 CTC fine-tunes of the Omnilingual ASR wav2vec 2.0 encoder (Omnilingual ASR team et al., 2025) (31 tokenizers at 300 M, nine at 1 B) that share encoder, training data, and training schedule, varying only the tokenizer: 13 orthographic tokenizers (character, BPE-512/1024/2048, syllable-512/1024/2048, multi-syllable with and without syllable separators) and 18 phoneme-track tokenizers (raw IPA, phoneme-BPE, phoneme-syllable, phoneme-multisyllable; vocabulary sizes 43–3,840). The matrix is large enough to dissociate vocabulary size from tokenization strategy. (2) We evaluate every model on the in-domain test set and on two structurally orthogonal OOD test sets: dialectal spontaneous speech (RVG 1) and standard-German negotiation dialogues (Verbmobil), quantifying when the phonological bias helps, where it stays competitive, and how its payoff depends on vocabulary size. (3) We complement aggregate WER with paired-bootstrap significance testing (Bisani and Ney, 2004) and confusion-matrix analyses; we focus on WER and CER rather than token-level metrics, since the latter conflate recognition quality with tokenizer compression rate and are not directly comparable across the very different vocabulary sizes (43 to 10,288 tokens) in our design space.

Headline result. Phonologically informed tokenizers cluster within the confidence intervals of BPE and character baselines in-domain. Under dialectal-phonetic OOD they show a small advantage that sits at the Bonferroni boundary (−1.09 pp WER on RVG 1-ORT, p=0.019, threshold α/k≈0.017 for k=3 tests on this dataset), and under spontaneous-lexical OOD a small lead (−0.89 pp WER for syllable-512 on Verbmobil), giving a characterization of the phonological-bias trade-off rather than a single “best tokenizer”. That trade-off runs along vocabulary size rather than along the type of shift: the advantage reverses on both corpora at |V |=2048 (§4.5).

## 2 Related Work

The choice of output unit in ASR has recurred as a design question through every architectural paradigm. Classical large-vocabulary systems combined pronunciation lexica, context-dependent triphones, and language models (Lee et al., 1990; Young et al., 1994), later composed through weighted finite-state transducers (Mohri et al., 2002). For German specifically, Killer et al. (2003) found that graphemes match phonemes in GMM-HMM systems, while Mousa et al. (2012) reported that morpheme-level language models lower out-ofvocabulary rates. Both findings are early evidence that linguistic units remain competitive in-domain, with conditional advantages elsewhere.

The unit question survived the neural transition. Hybrid NN/HMM systems (Bourlard and Morgan, 1994) retained phonemic states as targets; end-toend CTC reopened the question, with Graves et al. (2013) reestablishing phoneme-CTC on TIMIT. Sennrich et al. (2016) introduced Byte-Pair Encoding for neural machine translation, and Zenkel et al. (2018) adapted it to CTC ASR with a key contrast between Subword units (merges constrained to within words) and Crossword units (merges allowed across word boundaries). Their distinction maps directly onto our syllable vs. multisyl contrast at the syllable rather than word level.

Irie et al. (2019) compared graphemes, phonemes, and wordpieces on LibriSpeech and found graphemes and wordpieces dominate without an external language model; for German, Khosravani et al. (2020) found BPE and unigram subwords to outperform character- and word-level outputs (with n-gram LMs). Sainath et al. (2018) found that graphemes largely obviate the lexicon, whereas Zeineldeen et al. (2020) responded that phoneme-BPE with homophone disambiguation remains competitive. A separate line of work derives subword vocabularies from pronunciation information (Xu et al., 2019; Papadourakis et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2021). Most directly related to our phoneme track, Klumpp et al. (2022) released the Common Phone corpus and trained a wav2vec 2.0 phoneme recognizer covering six languages including German, achieving 18.1 % PER across 101 phonetic symbols; our phoneme-track models follow the same acoustic-modeling setup but vary the tokenizer.

Syllable-based ASR has a thinner literature. Ganapathiraju et al. (2001) demonstrated syllabic acoustic units in HMM-LVCSR on Switchboard; Qu et al. (2017) trained syllable-level CTC for Mandarin; and Manohar et al. (2023) applied Syllable-BPE to Malayalam, the closest cross-linguistic precedent to our German Pyphen-based syllable tokenizers (Liang, 1983; Pyphen developers, 2023).

None of these works evaluated tokenizers under structured domain shift, which is the gap our paper addresses.

A complementary literature on ASR robustness holds the tokenizer fixed and varies the acoustic side. Likhomanenko et al. (2021) established that single-corpus WER is a poor robustness proxy; Hsu et al. (2021) showed that encoder-side adaptation closes 66–73 % of the OOD gap in wav2vec 2.0; and Radford et al. (2023) framed robustness through weak-supervision scale. Our results are consistent with the encoder-as-primary-lever finding (confusion topology is largely tokenizerinvariant in our setup), but tokenizer choice acts as a complementary lever whose payoff depends on the OOD axis and vocabulary size. We extend this lineage by holding the encoder fixed and varying only the output tokenizer, isolating the tokenization effect from the lexicon, decoder, and training-scale variables that confounded earlier comparisons.

## 3 Methodology

## 3.1 Datasets

Training data is a 961-hour mixture of German Common Voice (Ardila et al., 2020) and the Spoken Wikipedia Corpus (Oct. 2017 release; Köhn et al., 2016; Baumann et al., 2019). Evaluation uses three structurally distinct test sets: the held-out portion of the same mixture (CV+SWC test, in-domain), BAS RVG 1 (Burger and Schiel, 1998), and BAS Verbmobil VM1 (Burger et al., 2000; Schiel, 1999). Table 1 summarizes the training mixture and the three evaluation sets.

The two OOD corpora are intentionally orthogonal. RVG 1 is spontaneous speech recorded across nine German dialect regions; it varies the phonetic realization while the underlying lexicon remains largely shared across regions. Verbmobil VM1 is spontaneous standard-German negotiation speech; it keeps the phonetic norm but introduces unfamiliar word-forms (hesitations, repairs, colloquial shortenings, discourse particles). Each corpus therefore stresses a different tokenizer property: phonetic robustness for RVG 1, lexical coverage for Verbmobil. Both provide parallel ORT (canonical orthography) and TR2 (dialect transliteration with prosody markers) reference tiers, which we report separately.

## 3.2 Tokenizer design space

We compare three classes of tokenizers, deliberately matched for vocabulary size where possible. Table 2 shows what the smallest variant of each family produces on a single illustrative example, which we discuss in §3.7.

## 3.3 Multilingual character inventory

OMNI-VOCAB is the publicly released omniASR\_tokenizer\_written\_v2 of Omnilingual ASR (Omnilingual ASR team et al., 2025), declared as tokenizer\_family: char\_tokenizer in the model card: a character-level tokenizer with ≈ 10,288 multilingual code-points covering 1,600+ languages, shipped as the default for all v2 architectures (CTC and LLM checkpoints from 300 M to 7 B). On our German training data, only 59 of these code-points occur more than 100 times each (top-50 coverage >99.99 % of all token occurrences); a long tail of ∼600 further code-points appears as rare orthographic artifacts. OMNI-VOCAB serves as the “no phonological bias” comparator inherited from the pretrained ASR model.

## 3.4 Data-driven subword

GERMAN-BPE is a standard SentencePiece BPE (Sennrich et al., 2016; Kudo and Richardson, 2018) fitted to the German training transcripts at vocabularies {512, 1024, 2048}. It serves as the datadriven comparator and is the de facto default in many modern German ASR systems.

## 3.5 Phonologically informed orthographic tokenizers

We construct three orthographic families on top of SentencePiece BPE (Sennrich et al., 2016; Kudo and Richardson, 2018), differing only in which symbols are protected as user-defined symbols (UDS) and whether the training text is presyllabified. SYLLABLE-k uses the Knuth–Liang hyphenation algorithm (Liang, 1983) via Pyphen (Pyphen developers, 2023) to syllabify the training transcripts; the syllable separator - is registered as a UDS so that no BPE merge crosses a syllable boundary. Vocabularies are k ∈ {512, 1024, 2048}. MULTISYL-k is trained on the same syllabified text, but the separator - is not protected, allowing merges to cross syllable boundaries; in addition, the 100 most frequent German syllables are preregistered as UDS so the model retains the most useful multi-character atoms even at small vocabulary sizes. This corresponds conceptually to Zenkel et al.’s (2018) Crossword variant but operates at the syllable rather than word level. MULTISYL-NOSEPk keeps the same 100-syllable UDS list as Multisyl but is trained on raw, non-syllabified text, so no syllable-separator token ever appears in the inputs. It isolates the effect of having syllables as atomic vocabulary entries from the effect of an explicit syllable-boundary marker.

## 3.6 Phonologically informed phoneme tokenizers

The phoneme track applies the same four merge strategies (raw, BPE, syllable, multi-syllable) to G2P-derived X-SAMPA phoneme sequences (Bisani and Ney, 2008). The G2P model is a jointsequence WFST built with Phonetisaurus (Novak et al., 2012) and trained on the German pronunciation lexicon shipped with MaryTTS (Schröder and Trouvain, 2003) (de.txt, ∼31k entries), giving canonical SAMPA transcriptions for every word in our training and test transcripts. Out-of-lexicon words are handled by Phonetisaurus’ default jointsequence backoff (beam size 20); compound forms are not pre-decomposed and are mapped as whole tokens. The 18 resulting phoneme-token configurations span vocabulary sizes from 43 (the smallest character-level raw variant) to 3,840 (the largest multi-syllable BPE). The phoneme target inherits the German MAUS/BAS tradition (Schiel, 1999) and parallels Common Phone’s wav2vec 2.0 phoneme recognition setup (Klumpp et al., 2022).

## 3.7 Tokenizer comparison

Table 2 compares the smallest variant of each tokenizer family on a single illustrative word, both in its orthographic form Unterhaltung (12 characters, four canonical syllables un-ter-hal-tung) and in its X-SAMPA transcription ?Un-t6-hal-tUN.

On the orthographic track, syllable-512 is the only family that recovers all four canonical syllables as individual tokens; German-BPE-512 finds three of them from data alone but splits the suffix tung into t | ung; the multisyl families pick finer, phonotactically motivated clusters that can additionally merge across syllable boundaries. On the phoneme track, phone-multisyl-lite-512 is the only family that captures all four SAMPA syllables as single tokens. The classes therefore differ in what kind of boundary they choose, not just in granularity; this distinction frames the cross-domain results in §4.

## 3.8 Evaluation protocol

We report token-weighted WER and CER for orthographic models and PER and CER for phoneme-track models, both with 95 % pairedbootstrap confidence intervals (CIs; $n _ { \mathrm { b o o t } } { = } 1 0 , 0 0 0$ seed=42, percentile method) (Bisani and Ney, 2004). For the orthographic track, references and hypotheses are normalized before scoring (lowercase, ß→ss, punctuation stripped, whitespace collapsed); this matches the standard Whisper-style protocol and ensures that capitalization conventions of the reference (Verbmobil business-style transcripts use Sie/Termin/Mai capitalization, while CV+SWC are lowercase) do not contaminate the recognition metric. Phoneme-track (X-SAMPA) evaluation is not normalized, since SAMPA symbols would be destroyed by punctuation stripping. Pairwise comparisons apply per-dataset Bonferroni correction. For the OOD corpora we pool train+dev+test (none seen at training time, which justifies pooling for tighter intervals at no leakage cost). Verbmobil VM1 contains English as well as German negotiation dialogues; we score its German subset (13,633 utterances, 33.8 h), identified from the reference transcripts by a German/English function-word criterion. Audio is 16 kHz mono and capped at 60 s for CV+SWC and Verbmobil (matching the external baselines); RVG 1’s fulllength recordings, the only ones routinely exceeding 60 s, are instead segmented at silence boundaries into ≤60 s chunks so the complete utterance is scored rather than truncated. Decoding is greedy CTC without an external language model: a tokenlevel LM would tie the decoding vocabulary to the tokenizer under study and conflate its languagemodeling contribution with the acoustic comparison. Every WER/CER/PER reported here therefore reflects the encoder plus tokenizer-specific head in isolation; LM rescoring, and the tokenizer–LM interaction it opens up, is left to future work (§5).

## 4 Experiments and Results

## 4.1 Setup

We train 40 CTC fine-tunes (Graves et al., 2006) of the Omnilingual ASR wav2vec 2.0 backbone (Baevski et al., 2020; Omnilingual ASR team et al., 2025): all 31 tokenizers (13 orthographic, 18 phoneme-track) at 300 M, and a nine-tokenizer subset at 1 B. The subset comprises the full vocabulary sweep of Syllable and German-BPE plus one variant per remaining family (omni-vocab, multisyl-512, multisyl-nosep-512). All runs use the Adam optimizer with a fixed learning rate of $5 \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 }$ bfloat16 mixed precision, no encoder freezing, 20 k optimization steps, and a batched dynamic budget of ${ \approx } 2 . 3 { \times } 1 0 ^ { 7 }$ audio elements per gradient step (min/max audio 2/60 s). Batching is audio-elementbased rather than token-based: every run sees the same audio in the same order (identical seed) for the same 20 k updates, and the batch size is never adjusted per tokenizer. The number of target tokens therefore varies with tokenizer fertility; we deliberately do not match it, since fixing the token count would unfix the audio budget and confound the comparison with the amount of acoustic evidence. Appendix C reports the resulting targettoken counts per run. The CTC output head is a single linear projection from the final encoder layer to the tokenizer’s vocabulary size (no layer selection was performed), with Xavier-uniform weight initialization and zero-initialized bias; no final-layer dropout is applied. Augmentation, label smoothing, validation schedule, and early-stopping criterion are held identical across all runs so that any crosstokenizer difference is attributable to the output unit and its head alone. We organize the empirical comparison around three research questions, each probing one of our three test sets, complemented by paired significance tests for nine focal comparisons.

The WER range is 14.9–18.0 % (German-BPE-512 best, multisyl-nosep-2048 worst); CIs have half-widths of 0.25–0.27 pp and overlap substantially across the top cluster: German-BPE-512, syllable-512, omni-vocab, syllable-1024, and the multi-syllable variants at vocabulary 512 are statistically indistinguishable from each other on WER. ## 4.4 Q3: Spontaneous-lexical OOD (Verbmobil)

Table 6 (right half) shows a much flatter picture. On the ORT tier, syllable-512-1b reaches the lowest WER at 21.27 %, ahead of multisyl-512-1b (22.05 %) and omni-vocab-1b (22.16 %), which are statistically indistinguishable from each other (C1-vm in Table 7); syllable-1024 leads on CER (13.51 vs. 14.08 for omni-vocab). All German-BPE variants and syllable-2048 fall between 24.17 and 26.95 %, at least 2 pp behind these leaders. The TR2 tier mirrors ORT throughout (0.5 to 1 pp higher, unchanged ordering). The systems in front are therefore the phonologically informed tokenizers at |V |≤1024 together with the character baseline, and syllable-2048 falls 4.3 pp behind syllable-512, the same direction as on RVG 1 (§4.5).

The shift on Verbmobil is driven by new lexical material rather than phonetic variation: hesitations (“Em”, “naja”), phonetic-shorthand verb conjugations (“müsten” for “müssten”), and discourse particles. A character tokenizer has no outof-vocabulary problem, whereas a syllable- or BPEbased vocabulary fails on these unseen forms via cascading substitutions (aligned examples in Appendix B). One consistent (though confounded with vocabulary type) interpretation is that finer-grained inventories simply have more recovery paths when no whole word matches: omni-vocab emits ≈ 6.9 tokens per word in our test data, multisyl-512 only ≈ 4.5. In aggregate WER this advantage is offset by the character inventory’s own failure mode of transcribing reduced function words literally, so on Verbmobil the two error types cancel rather than one dominating.

Pairwise significance tests on nine focal comparisons (Table 7, top block) confirm this picture: every comparison on CV+SWC passes Bonferroni correction at α=0.05; the syllable-vs-German-BPE comparisons on RVG 1 are strongly significant in both directions; and the multisyl-vs-omni-vocab comparison on RVG 1 sits at the Bonferroni boundary on the ORT tier (P1-ort) and fails significance on the TR2 tier (P1-tr2), as it does on Verbmobil (C1-vm). For absolute scale, Whisper-largev3 (Radford et al., 2023) and Parakeet-TDT-v3 (Sekoyan et al., 2025), both trained on two to three orders of magnitude more audio, reach 11.4 % and 11.9 % in-domain WER on the same CV+SWC test set (statistically tied with our best 1 B fine-tune at 11.7 %) but sit 5 to 20 pp ahead cross-domain. We report these numbers for context only; training-data scale is orthogonal to the tokenizer question and is not the contribution of this work.

## 4.5 Vocabulary size and tokenizer family interact

In-domain, both effects are small: marginal WER varies by 1.0 pp across |V | and by 1.2 pp across families, and |V|=512 is best or tied within every family. Under domain shift the two factors interact (Table 8). As |V | grows, German-BPE improves on RVG 1 while Syllable degrades, so the 8.5 pp lead of Syllable at |V |=512 turns into a 1.5 pp deficit at 2048; Verbmobil shows the same crossover (−4.2 to +1.4 pp). The paired-bootstrap interaction contrast is +10.0 pp on RVG 1 and +5.6 pp on Verbmobil (C2 in Table 7).

Granularity alone cannot account for this pattern. Finer tokenizers are better on average (Spearman ρ between characters per token and WER across the nine 1 B systems: +0.67 on RVG 1- ORT, +0.68 on Verbmobil-ORT), but Syllable is the finer tokenizer at every |V |, so a monotone effect of granularity predicts no crossover at all, and a granularity-matched pair still differs significantly on both corpora (C3). Undertraining of large vocabularies does not account for it either: with the audio budget fixed, Syllable-2048 receives 1.6× more target-token occurrences per vocabulary entry than German-BPE-2048 and still loses to it (Appendix C). We therefore read the crossover as a capacity effect: at small |V | the phonological prior supplies structure that BPE cannot afford to represent, whereas at large |V| BPE rediscovers syllable-like units on its own and the hard syllable constraint becomes a liability. C3 replicates on a second, architecturally different backbone, as do the directions of C1 and C2 on RVG 1 (mHuBERT-147; Appendix A).

## 4.6 Analysis: error patterns are tokenizer-invariant

We compute confusion matrices for all 18 phoneme tokenizers across all three corpora. The pattern is striking: across every tokenizer the top-5 indomain substitution pairs are the same bidirectional function-word swaps (“der”↔“die”, “und”↔“der”, “und”↔“die”), with maximum count differences between tokenizers ≤10 % for the 17 well-converged runs (the 40 %-PER outlier phone-multisyl-v2- 3840 shows the same ranking at inflated absolute counts). This invariance holds across representative extremes of vocabulary size and merge strategy (e.g., phone-char-raw, phone-bpe-1024, phone-multisyl-lite-512). One important caveat remains: the confusion counts are computed at the whitespace-separated SAMPA-word level rather than at the atomic-IPA-segment level (see Limitations). Atomic-phoneme confusion analysis is left for future work; we therefore read the present observation as indirect evidence that the encoder determines which errors occur on these data, while the tokenizer modulates how many. In §4.7 we show that this redistribution materially affects deployment WER under domain shift.

## 4.7 Discussion

The opening question, whether phonologically informed tokenizers built from German syllabification and grapheme-to-phoneme mappings might offer something that data-driven and multilingual defaults do not, admits a nuanced answer in three positive components.

Phonologically informed tokenizers are fully competitive in-domain. All top tokenizers cluster within their confidence intervals on CV+SWC, so adopting syllable- or phoneme-derived units incurs no in-domain accuracy cost while adding practical advantages: more interpretable confusion analyses, smaller embedding tables in production (relative to the 10,288-token multilingual character inventory), and a linguistically transparent design space.

Under domain shift, the choice of output unit becomes a useful tool whose payoff depends on vocabulary size rather than on the type of shift. When the shift is phonetic (RVG 1-ORT), the syllable-aware multisyl-512 takes the WER lead while omni-vocab stays ahead on CER; when it is lexical (Verbmobil), syllable-512 takes the lead with omni-vocab about one point behind and every German-BPE variant further back. The item-level mechanisms still move in the direction phonological theory would predict: syllable-aligned units anchor the model in intra-syllabic acoustic variation, and finer-grained inventories absorb out-ofvocabulary lexical material. On Verbmobil, however, they offset each other in aggregate WER. What separates the systems there is the size of the vocabulary: against German-BPE, the syllable prior wins by 4 to 8 pp at |V |=512 and loses on both corpora at $\lvert V \rvert = 2 0 4 8$ . Tokenization thus emerges as a deployment-time lever, but one tuned against the vocabulary budget as much as against the expected mode of variation.

The acoustic encoder and the output tokenizer act as complementary, not competing, design axes. Across all 18 phoneme-track tokenizers, the dominant error patterns are broadly similar bidirectional function-word swaps, indicating that tokenization redistributes errors along a shared encoder-fixed backbone, and this redistribution is large enough on domain-shifted data to materially change deployment outcomes. We therefore see linguistic structure not as an alternative to encoderside improvements such as domain-adaptive pretraining (Hsu et al., 2021) but as an orthogonal lever that adds value on top of them.

## 5 Conclusion

Across 40 CTC fine-tunes of the Omnilingual ASR wav2vec 2.0 backbone for German (31 tokenizers at 300 M, nine at 1 B), evaluated on three orthogonal corpora, the phonologically informed tokenizers (Pyphen syllables, multi-syllable merges, phoneme-derived units) are competitive with BPE and character baselines in-domain, provide the best system on both OOD sets at small vocabulary sizes while losing that lead at larger ones, and complement rather than replace the acoustic encoder, since the dominant error patterns are broadly similar across all 18 phoneme-track variants. Linguistic structure thus remains a useful design axis for German ASR, and combining linguistic priors with encoder-side adaptation is a natural next step, as is LM rescoring, whose interaction with the output unit we leave open.

## Limitations

Our largest models are 1 B parameters trained on 961 h of audio; external baselines trained on two to three orders of magnitude more data sit 5–20 pp ahead cross-domain (although in-domain WER is statistically tied), so the trade-offs we report could be partially compressed at production scale. We probe two structurally orthogonal OOD axes, dialectal-phonetic (RVG 1) and spontaneous-lexical (Verbmobil), and leave combined modes (e.g., dialectal negotiation speech), noise robustness, and code-switching to future work. All 1 B results in Tables 5, 6, and 7 use the continued-training checkpoints; the in-domain delta between initial and continued training is small (≤0.7 pp WER across the nine 1 B tokenizers), so the reported cross-domain effects are not dominated by undertraining of any single variant. The second-backbone replication (Appendix A) uses a 95 M base model with much higher absolute error rates, and the direction of the vocabularysize interaction C2 depends on encoder capacity. The phoneme-track confusion analysis operates on whitespace-separated SAMPA word transcriptions rather than atomic IPA segments, and the phoneme track itself emits phoneme sequences; a deployed system would additionally require a phoneme-to-grapheme decoder (Lee et al., 2023). Findings are reported for German; the dialectal-vsspontaneous trade-off is plausible for typologically similar languages (dialectally rich, syntactically free), but cross-linguistic generalization remains an open question we leave to future work.

## Ethical Considerations

The training data (Common Voice, Spoken Wikipedia Corpus) is released under permissive licenses and consists of recordings by consenting volunteer speakers. The OOD corpora (RVG 1, Verbmobil) are licensed via the Bavarian Archive for Speech Signals (BAS) for research use. The dialectal split of RVG 1 covers a balanced regional sample of German speakers; our evaluation does not infer or release any speaker identity. ASR systems can amplify existing inequities in recognition quality across speaker groups; our findings suggest that tokenizer choice can be tuned to mitigate this for particular OOD axes (dialectal speech in our case), and we recommend that practitioners audit their tokenizer choice for the specific deployment population.

## A Replication on a Second Backbone

To test whether the interaction structure of §4.5 is specific to the wav2vec 2.0 encoder, we repeat the experiment with mHuBERT-147 (Zanon Boito et al., 2024), a 95 M-parameter multilingual Hu-BERT base model that differs from Omnilingual ASR in self-supervised objective, architecture, and pretraining mixture. The encoder is the only change: the CTC recipe, training data, tokenizer artifacts, optimizer, learning-rate schedule, and audio budget of §4.1 are reused unchanged. Because mHuBERT-147 is a raw self-supervised checkpoint rather than an ASR-adapted one, the encoder is frozen for the first 10 k steps while the CTC head is trained (the standard HuBERT fine-tuning recipe; the setting is tokenizer-independent), and training runs for 30 k steps so that the encoder receives the same 20 k fine-tuning updates as in the main experiments. We train the eight systems that enter the contrasts C1–C3 and evaluate them with the protocol of §3.8.

Table 9 reports the three paired-bootstrap contrasts on both backbones; Table 10 gives the persystem WER. Absolute error rates are two to three times higher than at 1 B, as expected for a 95 M base model; the test is whether the sign structure replicates. C3 does so on both corpora, and C1 on RVG 1: multisyl-512 again beats omni-vocab on dialectal speech, and the granularity-matched pair again differs significantly on RVG 1 and on Verbmobil. C2 replicates on RVG 1 with a similar magnitude (+8.7 vs. +10.0 pp) but is negative on Verbmobil, where the same contrast is −0.9 pp on the 300 M wav2vec 2.0 fine-tunes as well: its direction tracks encoder capacity rather than the encoder family. Per-system rankings transfer poorly between backbones (Spearman ρ between the mHuBERT-147 and 300 M wav2vec 2.0 rankings of the eight systems: 0.00 on RVG 1, +0.57 on Verbmobil, +0.38 in-domain), which is what the interaction reading predicts: a ranking is not a property of the tokenizer alone. An interaction that survives an encoder swap cannot be a property of the wav2vec 2.0–tokenizer match.

## B Aligned Recognition Examples

Tables 11 and 12 illustrate the error types behind the cross-domain results, using the 1 B systems omni-vocab, multisyl-512, and German-BPE-512 (one per tokenizer class). Examples are selected automatically rather than by hand: we align reference and hypothesis at the word level, slide windows of 4–10 reference words over every utterance, and discard windows in which any system’s hypothesis collapses (hypothesis length outside 0.6–1.5 times the window length). On RVG 1 we keep windows in which multisyl-512 is error-free while every omni-vocab error is a near-miss substitution (character distance ≤2), i.e., a phonetic rendering of a dialectal realization; on Verbmobil (German utterances) we keep windows in which omni-vocab is error-free while both subword systems err on a word absent from the 6.14 M-word training text. Windows are ranked by the number of such errors, at most one window per utterance; one counterexample with the opposite pattern is shown per corpus.

The two corpora show different error types.

On RVG 1 the character inventory transcribes reduced dialectal forms literally (habe→hab, ist→is, und→un) and drops consonants inside function words, whereas syllable-constrained units restore the canonical form. On Verbmobil the subword systems fail on out-of-vocabulary compounds and names by producing plausible but wrong forms (arbeitsstreffen, anderthalbägiges), while the character inventory spells them correctly; the most frequent substitutions (Table 12), colloquial contractions such as wäre→wär and es→s, are shared by all systems.
