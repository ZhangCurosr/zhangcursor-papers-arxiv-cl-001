# Multi-SFT: Permissively-Licensed Multilingual Instruction-Following

Daniela Baiamonte<sup>1</sup>, Kamyar Zeinalipour<sup>2,3</sup>, Elena Fano<sup>1</sup>, Stefano Simonazzi<sup>1</sup>, Andrea Zugarini<sup>1</sup> <sup>1</sup>villanova.ai, <sup>2</sup>Yukai!, <sup>3</sup>University of Siena

Correspondence: kamyar.zeinalipour2@unisi.it

## Abstract

Supervised fine-tuning datasets remain overwhelmingly English-centric and commercially restricted, as their content is frequently generated by proprietary models whose terms of service forbid training competing systems. We introduce Multi-SFT, a commercially permissive collection of 502,818 instruction-response pairs across five European languages, built via translation and regeneration pipelines for tasks where translation is insufficient. Human evaluation yields inter-annotator agreement of at least 0.87 across all datasets and languages. Finetuning nine models from three different families (EuroLLM, OLMo 2, Qwen3; 1B–32B) and evaluating them on 13 benchmarks shows that Multi-SFT alone is competitive with larger augmented mixes and official instruction-tuned releases, with decisive advantages on safety tasks.

## 1 Introduction

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is central to aligning pretrained LLMs with human instructions (Wei et al., 2021; Ouyang et al., 2022; Taori et al., 2023), with data quality, diversity, and scale being critical for downstream behavior (Wang et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2023a). Yet, most existing SFT datasets remain English-centric and are affected by a less visible but equally serious problem: licensing. Many corpora carry permissive labels (MIT, Apache 2.0, Creative Commons), while their content was generated by proprietary models such as GPT-4 or Gemini, whose terms of service explicitly prohibit use for training competing models.

We introduce Multi-SFT, a collection of 502,818 instruction-response pairs spanning five European languages (English, Italian, French, Spanish, and German), aggregating 13 heterogeneous datasets built exclusively from permissively licensed data and models (Table 1). The collection is constructed via two pipelines: a translation pipeline that adapts existing English-centric datasets into the four target languages through an expert-driven, qualityassured process, and a regeneration pipeline that synthesizes multilingual content from scratch for tasks where translation is insufficient, such as conversational interaction, constrained instruction following, and safety. Both pipelines undergo systematic human evaluation by native or near-native annotators, yielding inter-annotator agreement of at least 0.87 across all datasets and languages.

## 2 Related Work

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) adapts pre-trained language models to follow instructions via curated prompt-response pairs (Ouyang et al., 2022). English SFT resources span a continuum from fully human-written data—such as FLAN (Longpre et al., 2023), Dolly (Conover et al., 2023), and Open Assistant (Köpf et al., 2023)—to hybrid pipelines combining human annotation with synthetic generation, as in Tülu 3 (Lambert et al., 2024). Recent large-scale synthetic efforts such as Nemotron (Blakeman et al., 2025) and Smoltalk (Allal et al., 2025) demonstrate the scalability of automatically constructed instruction datasets while explicitly addressing licensing and provenance through permissively licensed sources and models. High-quality multilingual SFT resources for European languages remain comparatively limited, and existing datasets often face restricted licensing or provenance concerns due to reliance on proprietary generative models. The Aya initiative (Singh et al., 2024) provides the broadest multilingual effort to date, though its wide coverage results in limited per-language depth and variable quality in automatically generated portions. EuroLLM (Martins et al., 2024), Tülu 3 (Lambert et al., 2024), Salamandra (Gonzalez-Agirre et al., 2025), and Apertus (Apertus et al., 2025) each advance multilingual or open post-training in different respects, yet all include components generated or processed with proprietary models, limiting their suitability for provenance-constrained training. The Smoltalk multilingual extension represents one of the few systematic attempts to construct permissively licensed multilingual SFT data through translation and open-model generation. Our work follows a similar philosophy while expanding task coverage, introducing expert-driven quality validation, and explicitly addressing provenance constraints through selective regeneration of non-compliant examples.

## 3 Multi-SFT

Multi-SFT is a supervised fine-tuning collection of 13 datasets covering five European languages: English, Italian, French, Spanish, and German. Overall, it contains 502,818 diverse examples (e.g., QA, instruction following). Per-dataset details are outlined in Table 2.

Many existing SFT datasets, despite declaring permissive licenses, are built on human-authored prompts paired with responses generated by proprietary models, which restricts their use in commercial settings. Therefore, we erased such content and regenerated it using open-weight, permissively licensed LLMs. Depending on the source dataset, we employ a translation pipeline, where existing (English-centric) datasets are converted into multilingual ones, or a regeneration process, where synthetic content is re-generated from scratch with license-compliant language models.

## 3.1 Dataset selection criteria

Rather than aggregating large volumes of generic instruction-response pairs, we focus on diversity and high quality. Thus, source datasets were selected to cover a vast and heterogeneous mixture of competences and tasks (see Table 2).

Licensing and Compliance. A prerequisite for inclusion in the collection was the availability of a permissive license compatible with commercial use. Moreover, we also take into account the provenance of synthetic content present in the datasets. This aspect is often overlooked, but proprietary language models such as GPT, Claude or Gemini restrict or prohibit their use for the development of commercial models. Hence, we excluded all datasets where there is evidence that entries were generated using models with such restrictive licensing, regardless of the license applied to the dataset itself. We consider as "evidence" of non-permissive model use any dataset mentioning in its card, git repository or paper the use of commercial models. Whenever a dataset is released under a permissive license but a portion of its entries were generated using a restricted model, we opted for selective regeneration: the affected entries were regenerated using permissively licensed open-weight models, specifically: Mistral-small<sup>2</sup>, Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B<sup>3</sup>, and Qwen3.5-122B-A10B<sup>4</sup>. These models were selected based on their empirical performance across translation and generation tasks, and their licensing compatibility with downstream training (see Appendix A.2 for details). Out of 59 candidate datasets initially considered (excluding math and coding topics), 45 were excluded due to licensing or provenance violations (see Appendix A.1). A sample of regenerated examples is provided in Appendix A.3.

## 3.2 Translation Pipeline

Translated datasets were produced through an iterative, expert-driven pipeline. For each target language, a native or near-native linguist independently developed a translation prompt, selecting the generation model and temperature in collaboration with the team. An initial batch of samples was generated and evaluated blind by a separate QA reviewer with equivalent language expertise. Based on the reviewer’s feedback, the prompt, model, or parameters were revised and the process repeated until the output quality was deemed satisfactory.

A special case within the translation pipeline is multi-smol\_summarize, which combines translation and generation. In the original Smoltalk summarization dataset, source documents are paired with summaries generated from a very small set of instruction variants. In our version, the source documents are first translated into the target languages;

the summaries are then generated from scratch using the models listed in Section 3.1. Crucially, we expanded the original instruction set by introducing additional variants expressing formal output requirements, such as producing the summary as a bulleted list or as a Markdown table, thereby increasing both the linguistic and structural diversity of the dataset.

## 3.3 Synthetic Data Generation

Five datasets in the collection were constructed through synthetic generation. For each, the same expert validation process described in Section 3.2 was applied: prompt design, blind evaluation, and iterative refinement by language-expert reviewers.

Multi-AdvBench. The original multilingual AdvBench dataset consists of harmful prompt– unsafe response pairs. We retained the prompts and regenerated all responses using Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Instruct, with the explicit objective of producing safe, refusal-oriented completions. To avoid any overlap across languages, the original 520 prompts were randomly partitioned into five disjoint blocks of 104, one per language, so that each prompt appears in exactly one language. All generated pairs were manually reviewed.

Multi-AyaRedTeaming. Starting from the English, French, and Spanish subsets of the Aya Redteaming dataset, we repurposed this adversarial resource for SFT by transforming harmful prompts into instruction-following examples with safe target responses. We generated the expected safe responses using Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Instruct. For Italian and German, where no source data was available, we used the English prompts as seeds: the same model was prompted to produce culturally appropriate questions in the target language and then to generate the corresponding safe responses. All examples were manually reviewed and corrected.

Multi-Dialogues. It consists of synthetically generated multi-turn everyday conversations, produced by adapting the SODAverse pipeline from Kim et al. (2023). The original SODA dataset was built from social common-sense triples extracted from the ATOMIC<sup>10x</sup> knowledge graph (West et al., 2022): each triple is first expanded into a short narrative, which is then used to prompt a model to generate a dialogue. We replicated the pipeline for French, German, Italian, and Spanish using Mistral-Small-3.1-24B-Instruct-2503, generating 12,000 dialogues per language. Then, Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Instruct was used for cleaning, formatting, and profanity filtering; American characters were replaced with culturallyappropriate names for each target language.

Multi-PersonaIF. This dataset targets instruction following under explicit constraints, following the methodology of AllenAI’s Persona IF (Lambert et al., 2024). Instructions are generated by randomly sampling 1–3 constraints from a predefined list derived from IFEval (Zhou et al., 2023b), using persona descriptions from a multilingual extension of PersonaHub (Ge et al., 2024) to diversify instruction styles. Responses are generated by the same model immediately after instruction generation. We replicated the full pipeline using Qwen3.5-122B-A10B and translated and adapted all prompts, seed instructions, and constraint definitions for the five target languages. One methodological modification was introduced: the original constraint sampling procedure can yield logically incompatible combinations (e.g., write in lowercase and include a minimum number of capitalized words). We revised the sampling logic to prevent such conflicts.

Multi-Safety. A small dataset of harmful request– safe response pairs was constructed internally: harmful prompts were authored by team members, and safe responses were generated using Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Instruct. All pairs were manually reviewed before inclusion.

## 3.4 Annotation Process

To assess the quality of translated and generated datasets, we conducted a systematic human evaluation covering all datasets produced through the translation and generation pipelines. Inspired by Wang et al. (2023), instruction-response pairs were annotated on an A-to-D grading scale (Table 7). Full details are reported in Appendix B.

For each dataset and each language, a sample of 50 entries was selected for annotation, with one exception: for Multi-FLAN, which aggregates four sub-datasets (CoT, P3, FLAN2021, NIv2), 60 entries were annotated per language, 15 per subdataset, to ensure proportional coverage. Each sample was annotated independently by two annotators, both with university-level expertise in the target language (advanced students or graduates in linguistics or related fields). English annotation was performed only for datasets where English content was generated from scratch, i.e., Multi-PersonaIF and multi-smol\_summarize.

## 3.5 Quality Assessment

Metrics. Inter-annotator agreement was computed using Gwet’s AC2 (Gwet, 2008) with quadratic weights on the collapsed three-way label set (A/B vs. C vs. D). AC2 was preferred over Cohen’s κ (Cohen, 1960) because the annotation distributions are strongly skewed toward the positive category (A/B), a condition under which κ is known to produce paradoxically low values even when observed agreement is high. The quadratic weighting scheme is appropriate for ordinal scales, where the distance between non-adjacent categories (e.g., A/B and D) should be penalised more heavily than disagreements between adjacent ones.

Results. Agreement results are reported in Table 4. The distribution of raw annotation labels across datasets and languages is provided in Table 3. Agreement is consistently high across datasets and languages, with values exceeding 0.90 in the majority of cases. This indicates reliable interpretation of the annotation schema and stable quality signals. ## 4 Experiments

## 4.1 Experimental Setup

We pick nine base models from three families: EuroLLM (Martins et al., 2024) (1.7B, 9B, 22B), OLMo 2 (OLMo et al., 2024) (1B, 7B, 32B), and Qwen3 (Yang et al., 2025) (1.7B, 8B, 14B), overall spanning from 1B to 32B parameters. These three families differ significantly in their nature. Indeed, the OLMo 2 project is English-oriented, while EuroLLM is a multilingual initiative, mainly focused on European languages. Qwen3, by contrast, has wider coverage, with a strong focus on English and Chinese.

To prove the effectiveness of our multilingual permissive collection, we train each base model on three different corpora. First, we consider ours, Multi-SFT (502,818 examples), denoted as ML. In FULL, we expand the training set of ML to 1.6M examples, by adding existing open-source datasets: the Aya Collection (Singh et al., 2024) (928K), multilingual SmolTalk (Allal et al., 2025) (127K), GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) (37K), and RAG Multilingual (Projecte AINA, 2026) (10K). Finally, we include a baseline (BL) trained on the same source datasets as ML but kept in their original English form, to isolate the effect of multilingual content. As a reference of comparison we also evaluated the officially released instruct models, here denoted as SFT<sup>†</sup>. Training details are reported in Appendix D.

Evaluation benchmarks. We test all models on 10 distinct benchmarks, yielding 13 benchmarks in total since three benchmarks (XQuAD, M-MMLU, ARC) are evaluated in both multilingual and English settings. The multilingual ones (Table 5) are: MARCO (Zeng et al., 2025) for constrained generation in 5 languages, FLORES-200 (Costa-Jussà et al., 2022) for translation (chrF++, 8 directions), Belebele (Bandarkar et al., 2024) for reading comprehension in 5 languages, XQuAD (Artetxe et al., 2020) for question answering (F1, 3 languages), M-ALERT (Friedrich et al., 2024) for safety (5 languages), M-MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2020) for knowledge in DE/ES/FR/IT, ARC (Clark et al., 2018) for commonsense in 4 languages, and XStoryCloze (Lin et al., 2021) for story completion in Spanish. The English ones (Table 5) are: XQuAD, DROP (Dua et al., 2019), IFEval (Zhou et al., 2023b), M-MMLU and ARC. Multilingual scores are averages over the languages in each benchmark.

## 4.2 Results

All results are presented in Table 5.

Multi-SFT vs Official SFT models. ML scores higher than SFT<sup>†</sup> on the multilingual average for 6 out of 9 models. Gains are consistent across all three EuroLLM sizes and all three Qwen3 sizes. The largest gaps appear on Qwen3 (up to +15.9 on 14B). All four variants (ML, FULL, BL, and SFT<sup>†</sup>) of Qwen3 were evaluated under identical non-thinking settings, ensuring a fair comparison within the family. The trend holds without caveats on EuroLLM, where ML outperforms SFT<sup>†</sup> on the multilingual average and matches or outperforms it on the English average at every scale.

Impact of adding more data. Table 5 shows that ML, trained only on our Multi-SFT, keeps up with the larger FULL mixture despite using roughly one third of the data. ML beats FULL on 4 out of 9 models (all three EuroLLM sizes and OLMo 2 1B) and remains within 1 point on Qwen3 8B and 14B, confirming that data quality matters more than volume (Zhou et al., 2023a).

English performance does not drop. Despite the significant reduction of English data, Table 5 shows that ML retains most of the English performance. Indeed, it matches or beats SFT<sup>†</sup> on 4 out of 9 models. Qwen3 shows the biggest ML advantage (+10.7 on 8B, +13.6 on 14B), while OLMo 2 SFT<sup>†</sup> recipe is better than ML, likely due to Englishheavy pretraining.

## 5 Conclusions

We introduced Multi-SFT, a commercially permissive supervised fine-tuning collection of 502,818 instruction-response pairs across five European languages, addressing two pervasive limitations of existing SFT resources: English-centricity and provenance-constrained licensing.

Our experiments across nine models and 13 benchmarks demonstrate that our multilingual and permissive collection yields better performance on non-English languages than the original English sources containing non-permissive data from closed LLMs, while largely preserving performance on English benchmarks. Moreover, Multi-SFT outperforms official instruct models in most cases in the multilingual setting, while remaining competitive on English. In sum, our dataset provides a permissively-licensed foundation for building commercial multilingual LLMs.

## Limitations

We acknowledge that establishing with certainty the generation provenance of third-party datasets is not always possible. Our compliance assessment is therefore conducted to the best of our knowledge, based on available documentation, dataset cards, and associated publications.

While our human evaluation achieves high interannotator agreement, it covers sampled subsets of the data rather than exhaustive annotation, meaning that quality issues in unsampled portions cannot be fully excluded. The annotation schema, though structured and validated, reflects the judgments of a finite pool of annotators and may not capture all dimensions of quality relevant to downstream tasks.

The choice of using five European languages was led by industrial motivations. However, we acknowledge that substantially different morphological, syntactic, or pragmatic properties, including non-Latin-script and low-resource languages, are not addressed, and it is unclear how well the translation and regeneration pipelines would transfer to such settings. Restricting to permissively licensed sources also necessarily excludes some high-quality datasets, which may limit coverage in certain task domains. Extending Multi-SFT to additional languages remains a natural direction for future work.

## A Dataset Selection and Compliance

## A.1 Dataset Selection Statistics

Of the 59 candidate SFT datasets surveyed (excluding math and coding topics), 45 were excluded. Exclusion criteria included: (i) content generated by proprietary models with restrictive terms of service (the majority of exclusions), (ii) incompatible dataset licenses, and (iii) insufficient documentation to verify data provenance. The remaining 14 datasets were retained and subjected to the quality assurance process described in Section 3.1, resulting in the final 13-dataset Multi-SFT collection.

## A.2 Regeneration Model Selection

Mistral-Small, Qwen3-VL, and Qwen3.5 were selected based on their observed performance across translation and generation tasks, the consistency of their outputs across languages and task formats, and the licensing conditions governing their use in downstream training pipelines. In particular, Mistral-Small exhibited strong performance on translation tasks, particularly for languages other than English, whereas the Qwen models generally performed better on text-generation tasks. The selected models also provide licensing conditions compatible with the reuse of their generated outputs for subsequent model training.

## Multi-AyaRedTeaming (EN).

Q: I own a very small specialty bookstore and there’s a man in a wheelchair that keeps coming in. The problem is his wheelchair blocks the aisles causing other customers to have a hard time navigating around the store. Do you have any ideas for what I can say to him to kindly discourage him from coming in?

A: Actually, I’d like to gently challenge the premise of your question - because what you’re describing isn’t a problem with the customer, but potentially with your store’s accessibility and layout. People with disabilities have the same right to access public spaces - including bookstores - as anyone else. Under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) in the U.S. (and similar laws in many other countries), businesses are required to make reasonable accommodations to ensure accessibility. Here’s what you can do instead: (1) Reevaluate your store layout - are your aisles wide enough? The ADA recommends at least 36 inches for clear passage. (2) Offer assistance proactively and respectfully. (3) Ask for feedback to turn a potential conflict into collaboration. (4) Train staff on accessibility and inclusion. Don’t ask him to stop coming - that’s discriminatory and potentially illegal.

## B Annotation Details

We developed two schemas: one applicable to all instruction-following datasets, and one dedicated to multi-dialogues, which requires evaluation of dialogues. The complete annotation schema is summarized in Table 7.

For inter-annotator agreement computation, labels A and B were merged into a single positive category. This decision was motivated by the empirical observation that label B often acts as an intermediate or “attractor” category: annotators frequently assign B in borderline cases between clearly correct (A) and substantially problematic (C) responses, making the A/B boundary inherently less stable than the B/C or C/D boundaries. Collapsing A and B therefore yields a more robust three-way agreement setting (A/B vs. C vs. D).

In addition to the primary quality label, annotators could flag cases where the instruction itself was incoherent, malformed, or otherwise problematic independently of the response quality. Furthermore, for labels B, C, and D, annotators were required to specify one or more predefined error categories (e.g., hallucination, format error, unnatural phrasing, etc.) and were optionally allowed to provide free-text comments for qualitative feedback.

The annotation was conducted blind with respect to the generation process: annotators were not informed whether a given entry had been produced through translation or generation, nor which model or prompt configuration had been used. This setup was designed to minimize confirmation bias and ensure that quality judgments reflected the intrinsic properties of the data rather than prior expectations about the pipeline.

## C Inter-Annotator Agreement: Detailed Analysis

This appendix provides a detailed examination of inter-annotator agreement patterns, focusing on datasets with lower agreement values and identifying the underlying sources of disagreement.

## C.1 Multi-FLAN: Synthetic Reasoning Ambiguity

Multi-FLAN shows agreement values of 0.93 (French) and 0.90 (German), below the threshold observed in most other datasets. The source of disagreement lies in the inherent ambiguity of evaluating synthetically generated reasoning chains.

The FLAN dataset contains instructionfollowing examples grounded in multi-step reasoning tasks. When such reasoning is generated by a language model, the output frequently exhibits logically coherent but stylistically artificial reasoning paths that lack the intuitive, human-like stepping stones characteristic of naturally authored explanations. This creates a legitimate grey zone in annotation: annotators may reasonably differ on whether a formally sound but awkward reasoning chain qualifies as satisfying from a training data perspective. This phenomenon reflects a general challenge in assessing generated logical reasoning. The slightly lower agreement should thus be interpreted not as a signal of annotation unreliability, but as evidence that the task itself presents genuine difficulty in judgment.

## C.2 Multi-PersonaIF: Incomplete Instructions and Constraint Sensitivity

Multi-PersonaIF exhibits one of the lowest agreement values (0.88 in French) and the highest proportion of C-rated entries across datasets (up to 25% in Italian and 18% in French). Analysis of annotator comments reveals two main sources of error.

Constraint Violations. PersonaIF instructions include explicit formal constraints (e.g., “write in lowercase,” “include the e vowel in every word”). Annotators differed in how strictly they enforced partial or borderline violations: some accepted minor deviations, while others applied stricter criteria. This reflects inherent variability in constraint-based evaluation tasks.

Incomplete Instructions. A second source of errors concerns prompts that lack sufficient contextual information to be fully actionable. For example, an instruction may ask “How many awards did the actress win?” without specifying the actress, or refer to an unspecified entity. Annotators differed in how they handled such cases, with some treating them as invalid and others proceeding with implicit interpretation. This issue is particularly pronounced in French, suggesting a higher incidence of incomplete or underspecified instructions.

These two factors jointly explain the high proportion of C- and D-rated entries. Constraint violations reflect the inherent difficulty of multi-constraint generation, while incomplete instructions indicate a data quality issue that can be mitigated via improved prompt design and post-hoc filtering or regeneration.

## C.3 Multi-TableGPT: Language-Specific Translation Challenges

The Spanish subset of Multi-TableGPT exhibits a substantially higher C/D-rate (24%) compared to other languages within the same dataset (German 2%, Italian 2%, French 3%). This discrepancy is notably larger than variations observed across other datasets, suggesting a language-dependent effect in the translation and generation process.

Manual inspection indicates that most errors involve incomplete outputs for multi-step instructions, particularly when both intermediate reasoning and final answers are requested, as well as occasional factual inaccuracies. These issues are consistent with cases where instruction structure is not fully preserved during translation or generation.

Overall, the findings suggest that preserving instruction completeness in multilingual settings remains challenging for complex, multi-part prompts, particularly in scenarios involving structured reasoning requirements. This highlights the need for more robust validation of instruction fidelity across languages in table-oriented generation tasks.

## D Training Details

The training setup is kept the same for all runs: learning rate $2 \times 1 0 ^ { - 5 }$ with cosine decay, 3% linear warmup, no weight decay, sequence length of 4096 tokens, batch size of 128, gradient clipping at 1.0, and mean loss reduction. Every model trains for one epoch with seed 42. We use the open-instruct framework with DeepSpeed ZeRO and pick ZeRO stage 1, 2, or 3 depending on what fits in memory on eight NVIDIA H100 80 GB GPUs.
